\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Simulation reservoir}

The reservoir is a fixed six-qubit circuit that turns each scalar input into
features. It sees the input twice, through two shallow reuploading layers,
which let a shallow circuit represent richer functions of the
input~\cite{perezsalinas2020data}. In each layer every qubit is first rotated
by an input-dependent amount, using $R_y$ and $R_z$ with a fixed per-qubit
offset. Neighboring qubits are then entangled by $R_{zz}$ couplings and mixed
by a small fixed non-Clifford rotation, keeping the circuit outside the
Clifford-only setting. All angles are drawn once and frozen, so the circuit
never changes during forecasting, and several independent frozen circuits
check that the results do not depend on one lucky draw. A single
computational-basis measurement then yields eleven features at once, the six
qubit signals $Z_i$ and the five neighbor correlations $Z_iZ_{i+1}$, at no
extra quantum cost.

Before forecasting, an exact noise-free simulation confirms the feature set is
useful. All eleven features respond to the input and are nonlinear above a
preset threshold, and they carry distinct information rather than copies of one
another. Because a few react similarly, their effective number is $5.15$,
somewhat below eleven. A leaky integrator with leak $0.2$ and the ten most
recent steps add temporal memory, and a ridge regression maps the features to
the next value, scored by test NRMSE. These checks show the reservoir supplies
a varied, nonlinear feature set. They make no claim of quantum advantage.

\subsection{Adaptive shot aggregation}

Write $x_t^{(n)}$ for the features produced by shot $n$ at time $t$. We split
the $N$ shots of each step into $G=N/k$ groups of equal size $k$ and average
within each group,
\begin{equation}
  x_t^{(g)}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{n\in g}x_t^{(n)}, \qquad g=1,\dots,G.
  \label{eq:grouping}
\end{equation}
Every group is a partly averaged view of the same time step, and all views
share the same target $y_t$. At test time we average the $G$ predictions of a
step, which is identical to predicting once from the averaged features. The two
methods are therefore scored in exactly the same way, so any difference between
EV and Grouped is created during training, not at test time.

The shot index carries no physical meaning, because shot $n$ at one step and
shot $n$ at the next come from unrelated circuit runs. We therefore shuffle the
shots freshly at every time step before forming groups, and we repeat the whole
analysis over several random groupings. A single grouping fixed across time is
kept as a sensitivity check.

\subsection{Fixed execution budget and training}

Evaluating $T$ time steps with $N$ shots per step costs $TN$ circuit
executions. We hold this budget fixed at ten thousand executions and study
three ways of spending it, $(T,N)=(250,40)$, $(500,20)$, and $(1000,10)$,
ranging from fewer time steps with more shots to more time steps with fewer
shots. For each setting we try every group size $k$ that divides $N$, which
includes Raw at $k=1$ and EV at $k=N$.

Selection respects time order. Using three expanding splits, each candidate
pair of group size and ridge strength $(k,\lambda)$ is fitted on an earlier
block and scored on the block that follows. The best pair is then frozen, the
readout is refitted on all data before the test block, and the final fifth of
the series is scored once. Normalization and feature scaling use training data
only, so the test block never influences any choice.
