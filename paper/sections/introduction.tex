\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Every quantum learning experiment produces a record of individual measurement
outcomes. Most learning pipelines average all shots from one time step into a
single expectation-value vector. This reduces measurement noise, but many
circuit executions then provide only one training example. Keeping each shot
separate creates more examples, but each example is noisy. We study the useful
range between these two choices.

Quantum reservoir computing (QRC) provides a controlled setting for this
comparison~\cite{fujii2017harnessing,nakajima2019boosting,mujal2021opportunities}.
A fixed quantum circuit transforms an input sequence into measured features,
and only a classical readout is trained. We can therefore keep the circuit,
targets, execution budget, and measurement record fixed while changing only
how the shots are combined.

Figure~\ref{fig:organization} shows the three representations. Raw keeps every
shot separate. EV averages all $N$ shots from one time step. Grouped divides
the same shots into disjoint groups and averages within each group. The
aggregation scale $k$ is simply the number of shots in one average. Thus,
$k=1$ gives Raw, $k=N$ gives EV, and values between them give several partially
averaged Grouped examples. We compare the settings on earlier time segments and
fix the best one before evaluating the final test period.

We call this procedure \emph{adaptive shot aggregation}. It treats the amount
of averaging as a readout setting and requires no additional quantum
executions. Across two synthetic systems and two measured series, Grouped
reduces test error relative to EV at the balanced budget on every task.
Matched controls show that grouping acts mainly as regularization of the
classical readout. Experiments on two superconducting processors show the same
positive trend. The study establishes when aggregation matters, why it helps,
and how it transfers from simulation to hardware.
