\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Adaptive shot aggregation recovers training structure lost by full averaging
without further quantum executions. It changes only classical processing: $k$
sets the shots per view, from Raw at $k=1$ to EV at $k=N$, and earlier blocks
determine $k$ and the ridge strength before test. The circuit and record remain
fixed.

The benefit is strongest when many shots are available at relatively few time
steps. Full averaging leaves few training examples; partial averaging yields
several denoised views whose differences expose unstable feature directions.
Equation~\eqref{eq:loss-decomposition} makes this exact: Grouped training is the
EV loss plus a covariance penalty derived from shot fluctuations.

Cov-EV applies the same type of penalty after full averaging. Its slightly
lower but closely matched errors support covariance regularization as the shared
mechanism and show that grouping is a convenient, but not unique, implementation.

The effect appears in the six-qubit simulation reservoir and a separate
ten-qubit circuit on two processors, testing transfer rather than qubit count.
Alternative scales require individual outcomes: expectation-only storage loses
this option, while retaining the shot record preserves it.

The hardware intervals cover one acquisition per task and processor and show
within-run stability, not cross-campaign reproducibility. Independent campaigns,
other reservoir families, and richer readouts are the natural next tests.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Turning a shot record into training data has always involved a choice, and the
field has answered it by reflex, averaging everything. We show the reflex is
beatable. Choosing the aggregation scale by validation lowers mean test error
on chaotic and real-world forecasting at the balanced budget, holds on two
superconducting processors, and requires no additional quantum cost. Because
every measurement-based reservoir produces such a record, the choice we make
explicit here is available to any of them, and the question it raises belongs
to finite-shot learning in general.

\appendices
\section{Reproducibility Summary}
\label{app:reproducibility}

All simulation tasks use the same six-qubit reservoir, preprocessing,
chronological selection procedure, and execution budgets. Mackey--Glass and
R\"ossler use five trajectories; Santa Fe and sunspots use five windows with
disjoint test blocks. Repeated settings are averaged within each unit before
uncertainty is computed. All methods share the raw record and ridge candidates.
The three budgets are $(T,N)=(250,40),(500,20),(1000,10)$, each totaling
10,000 executions. Hardware uses one fixed ten-qubit circuit and one 400-shot
record per task and processor, split into twenty disjoint $N=20$ blocks. The
supplement and \ifdefined\ANON artifact\else
\href{https://github.com/eybmits/split-ensemble-qrc}{artifact}\fi\ provide all
splits, settings, outputs, hardware job IDs, checksums, and rebuild scripts.
