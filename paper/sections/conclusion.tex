\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Adaptive shot aggregation is a small change to the classical side of a quantum
learning pipeline. The complete measurement record is retained, and each
aggregation scale $k$ decides how many shots are combined into each training
view. Earlier time blocks are used to choose $k$ together with the ridge
strength $\lambda$ before the test block is opened. Raw ($k=1$) and EV ($k=N$)
are included as the two extremes, so either conventional representation is
kept when partial averaging is not useful.

The practical advantage is that the method makes better use of measurements
that have already been acquired. It requires no additional
circuits, shots, mitigation, or trainable quantum parameters. Its largest gains
appear when a record contains many shots per time step but relatively few time
steps. In this regime, full averaging leaves only one training example for each
time step, while
individual shots are too noisy. Partial averaging provides several denoised
views of each target and lets the readout adapt to the available record.

Equation~\eqref{eq:covariance} explains this behavior. Training on grouped
views is mathematically identical to EV training with an added cost for relying
on feature changes that vary strongly across shots. Cov-EV applies this cost
directly and nearly matches Grouped. The gain therefore comes from stabilizing
the classical readout, not from creating additional physical information.

The same pattern appears across synthetic dynamics, measured time series, and
unmitigated hardware records. The feature audit verifies that the simulation
reservoir responds to the input, provides nonlinear information, and contains
no redundant measured feature. The separate
ten-qubit hardware circuit then tests whether the aggregation procedure
transfers to another reservoir design. It is not a qubit-count comparison,
but the positive trend on both processors shows that the method is not tied to
the six-qubit simulation circuit.

The method requires individual measurement outcomes because alternative
aggregation scales cannot be reconstructed from stored expectation values.
When bitstrings are retained, however, adaptive aggregation can be added after
execution and evaluated at negligible cost. The present study covers two
synthetic systems, two measured series, linear readouts, and one acquisition
per task and processor. Independent hardware campaigns and additional
reservoir families are the natural next tests.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Adaptive shot aggregation makes the representation of a finite measurement
record a controllable part of the learning pipeline. Across all four
forecasting tasks, intermediate aggregation reduced mean test error at the
balanced execution budget. Unmitigated measurements on both quantum processors
reproduced the positive trend. Equation~\eqref{eq:covariance} shows that
grouped views stabilize the readout using the observed shot fluctuations while using exactly the
same shots as Raw and EV. Retaining shot-level records therefore enables a direct
post-execution improvement without changing the circuit or increasing the
quantum execution budget.

\section{Reproducibility Summary}
\label{app:reproducibility}

All four simulation tasks use the same six-qubit reservoir, preprocessing,
time-ordered train-and-check procedure, and execution budgets. Mackey--Glass
and R\"ossler use five independent trajectories; Santa Fe and sunspots use five
windows with disjoint test blocks. Repeated circuit settings, measurement
samples, and grouping assignments are averaged within each trajectory or
window before uncertainty is computed. Every method receives the same raw
record and candidate ridge strengths. The three budgets are
$(T,N)=(250,40),(500,20),(1000,10)$, each totaling 10,000 executions. Hardware
uses one fixed ten-qubit circuit and one ordered 400-shot record per task and
processor, divided into twenty disjoint $N=20$ blocks. Complete time splits,
settings, raw outputs, hardware jobs, checksums, and rebuilding scripts are
provided in the supplement and artifact.
